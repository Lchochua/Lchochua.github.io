Human beings begin with partial interests and limited understanding, but depend on others. Interaction can generate moral and economic order without anyone designing the whole—provided justice restrains injury and institutions sustain the capacities needed for cooperation.

How to read this guide. This is a synthesis of the five supplied Smith documents. The framework is an interpretive reconstruction, not a formula Smith explicitly states. Moral judgment and market coordination remain distinct mechanisms.

References. TMS means The Theory of Moral Sentiments; WN means The Wealth of Nations. Reference badges identify the book and paragraph in the assigned readings.

01 · One framework, two domains

Partial people, mutual dependence

We begin close to ourselves, yet cannot live by ourselves. Both books ask how cooperation becomes possible under that condition.

  • Self-concern is natural, but does not exhaust motivation. Smith opens TMS with genuine concern for other people’s fortunes. Sympathy means fellow-feeling with any passion, not simply pity or generosity.TMS I.i.1.1 TMS I.i.1.5
  • Our understanding of others is indirect. We imagine their situations using our own experience. This makes sympathy possible while leaving it limited and uneven.TMS I.i.1.2 TMS III.3.4
  • Economic independence rests on social dependence. The ordinary labourer’s coat requires thousands of contributors. We need far more assistance than personal friendship could supply.WN I.i.11 WN I.ii.2

The shared question

How can people with limited knowledge and particular concerns live together in a sustainable and productive society?

02 · One framework, two domains

Learning to accommodate others

My desire alone does not establish a claim on another person. Cooperation requires me to attend to their standpoint or purpose.

In moral life, I examine conduct by imaginatively changing places with another person. I then learn to view myself through the eyes of a fair and impartial spectator. In economic life, I appeal to a trading partner’s advantage: an offer must give that person a reason to agree.TMS III.1.2 TMS III.1.5 WN I.ii.2

Price differences can encourage entry or withdrawal and adjust the allocation of resources, where movement is possible. This is a different process from judging whether a motive deserves approval.WN I.vii.12 WN I.vii.13 WN I.vii.14 WN I.vii.15

Keep the distinction. Understanding the baker’s interest helps me make an effective offer. It does not establish that my motives are virtuous. Likewise, a successful sale is not a verdict from the impartial spectator.

For discussion
  • Does taking another person’s interest into account necessarily mean caring about that person?
A possible answer
  1. No. An effective bargain may require understanding another’s purpose without benevolence. Moral judgment asks further questions about motives, propriety, and injury. The overlap concerns attention to others; it does not make all cooperation morally equivalent.

An interpretation for discussion, not a quotation from Smith.

03 · One framework, two domains

Justice: the indispensable foundation

Smith permits energetic pursuit of advantage within boundaries that protect other people from injustice.

“In the race for wealth, for honours, and preferments, he may run as hard as he can” — but he may not “justle, or throw down” his competitors.TMS II.ii.2.1

The corresponding condition in WN is explicit: a person remains free to pursue their interest “as long as he does not violate the laws of justice.” Justice frames economic liberty; it is not a benefit that commerce can substitute for.WN IV.ix.51

TMS already describes society subsisting through exchange among people without mutual affection. It also insists that injustice is destructive. This makes the butcher passage compatible with Smith’s moral psychology.TMS II.ii.3.1 TMS II.ii.3.2 TMS II.ii.3.3 TMS II.ii.3.4

Justice does not mean that nobody may ever lose a sale or a competition. Nor does the distinction from beneficence exclude all public provision: Smith allows a carefully limited public role in requiring mutual services, and assigns the sovereign duties in WN.TMS II.ii.1.8 WN IV.ix.51

04 · One framework, two domains

Motives and consequences are different questions

People can contribute to an extensive social result without intending or understanding it. The mechanism connecting action to outcome must still be explained.

Smith distinguishes efficient causes—what produces an action or sentiment—from final causes—the purpose it serves. The usefulness of justice does not establish that reflection on social usefulness originally produces our resentment of injury.TMS II.ii.3.5

Likewise, people did not invent exchange after calculating the prosperity that the division of labour would eventually create. The wider result develops through activities directed at nearer purposes.WN I.ii.1

The two invisible hands

TMS: necessities

Limited consumption by rich landowners, together with their expenditures, supports the distribution of necessities. Smith’s claim is not an equal distribution of wealth or power.

TMS IV.1.10

WN: domestic investment

Security encourages investment close to home; the pursuit of gain directs it toward valuable production. The argument has a particular setting in the discussion of import restraints.

WN IV.ii.5 WN IV.ii.9

Unintended does not mean beneficial

A social outcome needs an explanation and an evaluation. Emergence alone supplies neither a guarantee of public benefit nor a moral justification.

05 · One framework, two domains

When partiality acquires excessive authority

The processes that sustain cooperation can also distort judgment and concentrate power.

Public approval helps form moral judgment, but the desire to deserve approval is distinct from the desire to receive it. Conscience can challenge the crowd, yet its confidence can also be shaken by public condemnation.TMS III.2.1 TMS III.2.2 TMS III.2.32

In economic life, producers may gain by narrowing competition or securing monopoly. Merchants’ knowledge of their own interests gives them political influence without ensuring that their proposals serve society.WN I.vii.26 WN I.vii.28 WN I.xi.p.10

The comparison also has a limit: markets register demand supported by purchasing power. A market signal does not establish whose needs matter most morally.WN I.vii.8

For discussion
  • Can a profitable and popular activity still fail Smith’s moral test?
A possible answer
  1. Yes. Profit and applause answer different questions from justice and praiseworthiness. We must examine whether the activity violates rights, relies on privilege, or wins approval through mistaken judgments. Neither commercial success nor popularity settles those questions.

An interpretation for discussion, not a quotation from Smith.

06 · One framework, two domains

Society forms the people who sustain it

Individuals create social arrangements, and those arrangements develop—or damage—their capacities. This feedback completes the framework.

In TMS, society supplies the mirror through which we learn to examine ourselves. In WN, habit, education, and occupation help produce differences of talent that can appear natural.TMS III.1.3 WN I.ii.4

The division of labour can increase skill in a narrow task while impoverishing general understanding and the capacity for generous or tender sentiment. Greater productive power and diminished human capacities can coexist.WN V.i.f.50 WN V.i.f.51

Public education addresses a failure of ordinary commercial life to develop the capacities society requires. Smith supports basic instruction and values intellectual development even apart from its advantages to the state.WN V.i.f.49 WN V.i.f.54 WN V.i.f.55 WN V.i.f.61

An interpretive connection. Because moral judgment involves imagination and perspective-taking, the mental narrowing described in WN may threaten capacities central to TMS. Your commentaries suggest this connection cautiously. Smith does not explicitly derive his education policy from the impartial spectator in these passages.

07 · One framework, two domains

Putting the framework to work

Read a moral practice or an economic institution by tracing purposes, accommodation, rules, consequences, and the formation of people.

The central proposition

People’s particular purposes can sustain cooperation when interaction accommodates others, justice restrains injury, and institutions preserve the capacities cooperation requires. The same processes can undermine those conditions, making criticism and public action part of the account.

TMS explains how people become capable of judging and restraining themselves. WN explains how their activities become interdependent and productive, and how the resulting arrangements can strengthen or damage them.

For discussion
  • A factory increases output while making work more repetitive. What does the unified framework ask us to assess?
A possible answer
  1. Assess the gains in production and the distribution of their benefits, the conditions of exchange and justice, and the effects of work on general human capacities. Smith’s education argument shows why increased output alone cannot settle the evaluation. This does not imply that specialization should simply be abolished.

An interpretation for discussion, not a quotation from Smith.
