01 · R Pref.1–3

Who receives the produce?

Ricardo starts with a division: the produce of land, labour and capital becomes rent, wages and profit. His question is how the proportions change as society develops.

  • Three income sources. Landlords own the land; capitalists advance stock; labourers perform the work. The categories distinguish claims on production, not three kinds of human character. R Pref.1
  • Distribution changes with circumstances. Soil, accumulation, population, skill and agricultural instruments affect the shares. There is no fixed formula assigning each class the same proportion forever. R Pref.2
  • A system of connected questions. Value, rent, wages and profits explain one another’s movements. The broader lecture notes describe Ricardo’s abstract method; the Preface supplies the organizing problem. R Pref.3
For discussion
  1. Does greater national production guarantee improvement for each class?
  2. What does this way of dividing society reveal—and leave out?
Possible answers

No. An increase in the total does not specify its distribution. The machinery chapter later gives a concrete case in which the owner’s return is preserved while employment contracts.

It makes conflicts visible. It separates income sources, but real households can receive wages, profits and rent together. That is a limitation when applying the categories today.

In plain language · explanatory illustration

A larger harvest does not tell us who gains

Imagine that a society produces more grain than it did last year. That tells us something about its productive capacity. It does not yet tell us whether workers eat better, landlords receive more rent, or employers earn more profit.

Ricardo asks us to follow the claims on the harvest. Workers receive wages for their labour; owners of land receive rent; those advancing capital receive profit. The same increase in production can affect these incomes very differently.

Suppose food becomes harder to produce as more people must be fed. More grain may be grown overall, yet superior land becomes more valuable to rent, workers need higher money wages merely to buy their existing necessities, and employers face lower profits. We will build that argument in the following sections.

Keep this question in view: “The country is richer” is only the beginning. Who receives the additional produce, and whose livelihood improves?

02 · R I.1–23

Labour explains value—with qualifications

Usefulness is necessary for exchange value, but Ricardo does not treat it as a measure of value. For reproducible goods under competition, he looks to the labour required to produce them.

  • Scarce objects are a separate case. Rare pictures, coins and certain wines cannot simply be reproduced through more labour. Their prices depend on scarcity and buyers’ circumstances. R I.2–6
  • Count indirect labour. Weapons, ships, buildings and machinery carry labour into the final product. The stockings example follows the chain from cotton cultivation to retail. R I.11–14
  • Production time matters. Equal quantities of labour can yield different prices when capital is advanced for different periods. Distribution also changes relative values when industries use capital differently. R I.15–23
For discussion
  1. Does a commodity become more valuable merely because workers receive higher wages?
  2. Does Ricardo claim labour alone explains every observed price?
Possible answers

Not as a general rule. A change in wages alters distribution, and can change relative prices through different capital structures. It is not equivalent to an increase in labour required.

No. Scarcity exceptions, differences in capital and production time, and the distinction between normal and market conditions matter. His own qualifications belong inside the theory, not in a footnote dismissing it.

In plain language · explanatory illustration

The same labour, but a longer wait for the return

The £2,310 versus £2,200 example qualifies a simple labour account of value. Assume that wages are the same for both producers, so spending the same total on labour purchases the same quantity of labour. Each spends £2,000, and the normal annual profit rate is 10%. The difference is when the money is advanced.

  1. Producer A works over two years. The first £1,000 remains committed to production for two years; another £1,000 is advanced for the final year. The first advance must yield £1,210 and the second £1,100 when the product is sold: a total of £2,310.
  2. Producer B completes production in one year. All £2,000 is advanced for one year. A 10% return requires a selling price of £2,200.

A: £1,000 × 1.12 + £1,000 × 1.1 = £2,310
B: £2,000 × 1.1 = £2,200

The extra £110 is not payment for extra labour. It allows the first producer to earn the same annual rate of return while waiting longer. If both products sold for £2,200, the longer process would offer a lower annual return, giving capital an incentive to move elsewhere.

This uses the mathematics of compound interest: the first £1,000 must grow to £1,100 after a year and to £1,210 after two years. Ricardo is discussing the normal profit on capital committed to production; the example does not require an actual bank loan or a cash payment at the end of the first year.

The point: counting the labour required is not always enough to explain exchange value. The timing of the advances and the period for which capital is committed matter too.

03 · R II.1–15

Dear corn produces rent

Rent arises when equal outlays of capital and labour produce unequal returns. Competition transfers the differential advantage of better land to its owner.

  • The margin pays no rent. When abundant good land is free to cultivate, there is no reason to pay an owner for another equivalent plot. Bringing inferior land into use creates a differential surplus on the better plots. R II.3–6
  • The intensive margin works too. Later applications of capital on the same land can yield less. The last necessary application pays no rent even when earlier applications generate a surplus. R II.7–10
  • Follow the direction of causation. Production on the least favourable conditions needed to meet demand regulates value. Giving up rent does not remove those production requirements. R II.11–15

Ricardo’s yields of 100, 90 and 80 quarters are net of maintaining labour, for equal capital outlays. This example isolates differential rent; the slider does not calculate changing corn prices, wages or national income. R II.6

For discussion
  1. If landlords waive their rents, must bread become cheaper?
  2. Can rent arise without moving to a new plot?
Possible answers

Not in this argument. Waiving rent changes who receives the surplus, without reducing the labour needed at the regulating margin.

Yes. Additional capital on existing land can have diminishing returns. Rent attaches to the differential productivity of earlier applications, not only to differences between plots.

In plain language · explanatory illustration

What would you pay for better land?

Put yourself in the position of a farmer deciding how much to pay for a plot. In a simplified illustration, the same spending on workers, tools and seed yields 100 sacks on good land and 80 sacks on poorer land. The poorer land is freely available, and its 80 sacks cover your costs plus the normal profit you could earn elsewhere.

The owner of the good land asks for rent. How much could you pay? Up to 20 sacks. After paying 20, you retain the same return as on the free poorer land. At a lower rent, good land offers an extra gain, so competing farmers have an incentive to bid more for it. Competition allows the landlord to collect the productive advantage.

How does that rent appear?

  1. Good land is initially abundant. Nobody pays rent when an equally good plot is available for free.
  2. Demand for food grows. Once the good land is fully used, poorer land must also be cultivated. Grain must sell at a price that makes production there worthwhile.
  3. At that price, better land earns a surplus. It produces more with the same outlay. Its owner can collect that difference as rent.

Now apply the logic to the guide’s three yields, 100, 90 and 80. When the 90-quarter land is the necessary rent-free alternative, the best land has a 10-quarter advantage. When the 80-quarter land is also needed, the advantages become 20 and 10 quarters. The last necessary land earns no rent. Its farmer still earns the normal return on capital; “no rent” does not mean “no income”.

The same principle applies to extra investment on existing land. If a later application of capital yields less than an earlier one, the last necessary application sets the margin; the more productive applications yield a differential surplus.

Ricardo reverses the everyday explanation: bread is not expensive because landlords charge rent. Landlords can charge rent because producing the food society needs requires more costly conditions at the margin. Waiving rent would initially give the surplus to tenant farmers; it would not remove the need to cultivate the inferior land.

04 · R V.1–12; VI.1–8

The wage is a livelihood, not just a number

The natural wage sustains workers and their families at a customary standard. The market wage responds to labour demand and supply. Neither should be confused with a fixed biological minimum.

  • Custom shapes necessity. Food, housing and conveniences considered essential vary across places and periods. Ricardo explicitly rejects a constant real subsistence bundle. R V.1–2R V.9
  • Population supplies an adjustment mechanism. High wages can encourage population growth, which later puts downward pressure on wages. Yet continuing accumulation can keep market wages above the natural level indefinitely. R V.5–7
  • Dearer necessities squeeze profits. If producing food requires more labour and wages rise to meet its higher cost, agricultural and manufacturing profits fall. This is a conditional account of distribution, not a claim that every money-wage increase under every technology lowers national welfare. R VI.2–8
For discussion
  1. Can workers enjoy high wages for a long time in Ricardo’s system?
  2. Does a return to the natural wage undo the harm of unemployment?
Possible answers

Yes. R V.7 allows a continuing demand for labour to keep market wages above the natural rate for an indefinite period.

No such conclusion follows. An eventual adjustment in population or wages does not compensate the people who experience deprivation during it. This is an ethical question raised by the mechanism.

In plain language · explanatory illustration

A higher money wage can buy exactly the same livelihood

Suppose a worker’s customary weekly necessities cost £10. Food becomes harder to produce, and the same basket now costs £12. A wage increase from £10 to £12 leaves the worker able to buy exactly the same basket. The money wage has risen; the worker’s purchasing power has not.

This makes Ricardo’s distinction easier to see. The market wage is what workers actually receive, responding to employers’ demand for labour and the available supply of workers. The natural wage supports workers and their families at the customary standard of their society. That standard includes socially established necessities, not only enough food to avoid starvation.

The employer now needs £2 more to maintain the same worker. With the relevant production conditions held fixed, the adjustment leaves less profit. In Ricardo’s argument, employers cannot simply restore the old general profit rate by raising every price: a general wage increase is not itself an increase in the labour required to produce every commodity.

Connect this to rent. As inferior land becomes necessary, landlords gain rent on superior land. Workers may receive higher money wages merely to preserve their livelihood, while capitalists face lower profits. The same development affects the three classes differently.

Actual wages need not sit at the natural level at every moment. If investment keeps increasing demand for workers, market wages can remain above it for a long time. Nor is the customary standard frozen forever.

Always ask: has the wage risen in money, or has the worker’s ability to obtain necessities and enjoyments improved?

05 · R VI.9–10; VII.3–4

Why accumulation can slow

As more food requires increasingly costly production, the return to capital can fall until further accumulation no longer seems worthwhile. Ricardo presents a tendency with counteracting forces.

  • Accumulation needs a return. Investment stops before the limiting case in which profits disappear entirely. Risk and trouble require compensation. R VI.9–10
  • Improvement can check the tendency. Better agricultural methods or machinery that cheapens necessities reduce the labour required to sustain workers. R VI.9
  • Trade matters through the wage basket. Cheap imported food and other necessities can raise profits by reducing the cost of maintaining labour. Cheaper luxuries increase enjoyment without necessarily changing the general profit rate. R VII.3–4

A conditional tendency

The chain holds production conditions and the relevant adjustment mechanisms in view. Ricardo himself identifies technical improvement and access to cheaper necessities as counterforces. His stationary state is not a dated forecast of when growth must end.

For discussion
  1. Why might cheap wine and cheap grain have different effects on profits?
  2. What would make this account a poor guide to a modern economy?
Possible answers

Who consumes the good matters. If a cheaper commodity enters the customary wage basket, it can lower the wage cost. A luxury consumed only by the rich need not do so.

Test the mechanisms. Agricultural technology, demographic behaviour, wage institutions and access to imports can all change. The lecture notes discuss later criticism; these modern questions are not additional claims by Ricardo.

In plain language · explanatory illustration

Why would an employer stop expanding?

Imagine an employer deciding whether to save some income and expand production. Expansion is attractive when the expected return compensates for the risk and trouble of committing more capital.

Now connect the earlier sections. More workers and more production require more food. If obtaining it means cultivating poorer land or using less productive additional applications of capital, necessities become more costly. Wages must adjust to maintain workers, and the general profit rate is squeezed. Eventually, another investment may no longer seem worthwhile.

Accumulation can therefore slow before profit reaches zero. The relevant question is whether the remaining return is sufficient to induce further saving and investment.

What can interrupt this chain?

Better farming methods, useful machinery or access to cheaper imported food can make workers’ necessities easier to obtain. That relaxes the wage-cost pressure and can support profits and renewed accumulation.

This also explains why cheap grain and cheap luxury wine can have different effects. If grain enters workers’ customary consumption, making it cheaper can reduce the cost of maintaining labour. If a luxury is consumed only by the rich, its cheaper price improves their enjoyment without necessarily changing that cost or the general profit rate.

The stationary tendency is conditional: it follows from a particular chain of food costs, wages and profits. Ricardo also identifies changes that can break that chain.

06 · R VII.1–15

Trade depends on comparative costs

Portugal can require less labour for both goods and still gain from importing English cloth. The decisive comparison is each country’s internal cost of one good in terms of the other.

  • The four numbers. England needs 100 workers for cloth and 120 for wine; Portugal needs 90 and 80. Read them as labour for the specified quantities in Ricardo’s example. R VII.11–12
  • Compare ratios, not rankings. Wine costs 1.2 units of cloth in England and 8/9 in Portugal. Exchange between those ratios can save labour in both countries. R VII.11–13
  • Capital mobility is part of the argument. Ricardo distinguishes easy movement within a country from impediments to moving abroad. He explicitly considers relocation of English production to Portugal. R VII.8R VII.13–15

  2. Does international capital mobility make every argument for trade false?
Possible answers

Yes. England saves labour by exchanging cloth for wine even though Portugal uses fewer workers for either good. The terms must lie between the two domestic opportunity-cost ratios.

No. It changes the allocation problem and whose gains must be considered. Ricardo’s discussion makes the mobility assumption visible; it does not settle every modern case involving investment, migration and distribution.

In plain language · explanatory illustration

Why buy from a country that is less productive?

Portugal needs fewer workers than England to make either good. Why would it ever import English cloth? Because Portugal’s workers have an even greater advantage in wine.

Take an exchange of one unit of cloth for one unit of wine. England can make its own wine with 120 workers, or employ 100 workers making cloth and exchange it for that wine. Trading saves England 20 worker-years.

Portugal can make its own cloth with 90 workers, or employ 80 workers making wine and exchange it for that cloth. Trading saves Portugal 10 worker-years. Portugal is more productive in both activities, yet both countries save labour.

The comparison is between alternatives within each country. For England, obtaining wine through cloth is cheaper than making wine. For Portugal, obtaining cloth through wine is cheaper than making cloth. Absolute rankings across countries do not settle these choices.

Use the slider to change the bargain. If England gives 1.1 units of cloth for one unit of wine, it uses 110 rather than 120 worker-years. Portugal uses 80 worker-years for wine instead of the 99 needed to make the 1.1 units of cloth it receives. Both still gain, but the division of gains changes. At either endpoint of the mutually beneficial interval, one country’s saving disappears.

This reasoning takes the production requirements as given and keeps labour and capital from simply relocating across countries to reproduce Portugal’s costs everywhere. Ricardo explicitly discusses the international mobility of capital.

The question is not: “Who makes this most efficiently?” It is: “What must each country give up to obtain it by production rather than exchange?” The example shows scope for mutual gains; it does not determine which bargain will prevail.

07 · R XXXI.1–22

The owner can prosper while workers lose

Ricardo revises his earlier belief that labour-saving machinery necessarily benefits all classes. Net produce can be maintained while gross produce and the fund employing workers fall.

  • Follow the balance sheet. Initially £7,000 is fixed capital and £13,000 circulating capital. After building the machine, fixed capital is £14,500 and circulating capital £5,500. Total capital remains £20,000, with £2,000 profit. R XXXI.5–9
  • Why workers object. The same private return can accompany a smaller demand for labour. Their opposition is not simply ignorance about productivity. R XXXI.3–4R XXXI.16
  • Recovery is possible, not automatic compensation. Saving may rebuild employment, and gradual invention differs from the sudden change used in the example. Ricardo nevertheless continues to encourage machinery, including because discouraging it can send capital abroad. R XXXI.10–11R XXXI.18–22
For discussion
  1. Does a larger net produce establish that society as a whole is better off?
  2. What can this argument tell us about AI?
Possible answers

Not by itself. Ask whose income, employment and consumption improve, how losses are distributed, and whether eventual gains reach the people displaced. The numerical example separates private return from workers’ livelihood.

It supplies questions, not a prediction. Examine substitution, new tasks, the allocation of investment and the speed of adjustment. An AI comparison extends Ricardo’s mechanism; it is not evidence that his numerical example forecasts today’s labour market.

In plain language · explanatory illustration

A machine can preserve profit while shrinking the employment fund

Imagine an employer with £20,000 of capital. Initially, £7,000 is tied up in fixed capital and £13,000 circulates through the production process, supporting employment and the recurring advances it requires.

After the machine is built, £14,500 is fixed capital and only £5,500 remains as circulating capital. The total is still £20,000, but its composition has changed: £7,500 more is tied up in fixed capital, and £7,500 less remains in the circulating fund.

In Ricardo’s example, the employer’s annual profit remains £2,000. From the owner’s perspective the investment can therefore be satisfactory, even though the smaller circulating fund supports less employment. The machine need not raise the owner’s profit to make the distributional problem visible; preserving it while workers lose employment is enough.

That is why workers’ opposition need not be ignorance or hostility to progress. They can correctly see a threat to their livelihood that is not captured by the owner’s net return.

Ricardo allows saving, new investment and gradual adjustment to rebuild labour demand. But a later recovery does not itself compensate those who lose work now. His argument explains how workers can lose; it is not a fixed numerical prediction for every machine or every period.

Ask two separate questions: is the investment worthwhile for its owner, and what happens to the people whose employment it changes? The first answer cannot stand in for the second.

08 · A closing comparison with Smith

From making wealth to dividing it

Ricardo changes the centre of gravity of political economy. But Smith already asks who benefits, and Ricardo does not abandon the possibility that trade and improvement can enrich society.

  • A shift of emphasis, not a clean break. Smith’s pin factory and market-size argument foreground productive cooperation. Ricardo’s Preface makes distribution the principal problem. Smith also divides income among classes, so distribution does not begin with Ricardo. WN I.i.3WN I.iii.1WN I.xi.p.7R Pref.3
  • Trade: extend the comparison. Smith’s make-or-buy argument stresses alternative uses of capital. Ricardo demonstrates gains when Portugal is more productive in both goods. Smith’s passages contain opportunity-cost reasoning; they do not state Ricardo’s four-number result. WN IV.ii.11–12R VII.11–12
  • Progress has costs in both books. Smith connects repetitive work to diminished understanding; Ricardo connects machinery to lost employment. Neither concern is answered merely by showing that more can be produced. Their mechanisms, and the responses they explicitly discuss, differ. WN V.i.f.50WN V.i.f.55R XXXI.3–4
  • Private gain is not an automatic public verdict. Smith scrutinizes merchants’ policy proposals and the costs of removing protection. Ricardo separates capitalists’ net return from workers’ employment. The shared question is whose interest an argument about national prosperity actually serves. WN I.xi.p.10WN IV.ii.44R XXXI.9

The question to carry forward

Smith helps us ask how cooperation expands what society can produce. Ricardo insists that we also ask how the resulting gains and losses are divided. A satisfactory account of progress needs both questions.

For discussion

  3. What would a comparison miss by calling Smith optimistic and Ricardo pessimistic?
Possible answers

Both interpretation and emphasis matter. Smith explains the productive and institutional possibilities of commercial cooperation; Ricardo traces constraints and distributional shifts. Both texts recognize benefits and costs, but organize their explanations differently.

That is too simple. The broader Smith notes already identify rent as a consequence of price. Ricardo makes the regulating no-rent margin and differential mechanism central. Distinguish development of an argument from reversal of its conclusion.

It hides their internal tensions. Smith’s education and producer-interest passages qualify a reassuring account of commerce. Ricardo’s discussion of cheaper necessities and improvement qualifies the stationary tendency. Compare specific mechanisms and textual claims rather than assigning each author a mood.

In plain language · explanatory illustration

Ask about both the size of the pie and its division

Imagine a pin factory adopting a more elaborate division of labour. Smith helps us see how specialization, coordination and access to a larger market can increase what its workers produce together.

Ricardo directs our attention to a further question: how are the resulting incomes divided, and how does that division change? More production does not by itself tell us what happens to wages, rent and profit.

The distinction is a shift of emphasis, not a claim that Smith ignores distribution or that Ricardo thinks improvement is futile. Smith also examines class interests and the human damage of narrow repetitive work. Ricardo also explains gains from trade and the benefits of cheaper necessities.

Compare their concerns about the worker. In Smith’s repetitive-work argument, a worker may remain employed yet lose opportunities to exercise judgment and develop understanding. In Ricardo’s machinery example, the worker may lose employment while the owner’s return is preserved. These are different harms; an increase in output alone does not answer either one.

Carry both questions forward: how does society become able to produce more, and how does that change the livelihoods, powers and opportunities of different people?
